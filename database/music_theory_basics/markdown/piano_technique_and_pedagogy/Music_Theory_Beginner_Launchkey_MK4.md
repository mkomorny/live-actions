# Music Theory for Absolute Beginners  
## A Progressive Course with the Novation Launchkey MK4 61

**Document type:** Course knowledge base  
**Audience:** Absolute beginners  
**Practice instrument:** Novation Launchkey 61 (MK4)  
**Scope:** Western tonal fundamentals + hands-on controller practice  

**Truthfulness policy:** Every theory claim in this document is drawn from established open textbooks (primarily *Open Music Theory*) or official Novation documentation. Where sources differ (for example octave numbering of middle C), the difference is stated explicitly. Nothing here invents “rules” that sources do not support.

---

## How to use this course

### Course design principles

This course is organized as **progressive modules**: each module names a topic, states measurable learning objectives, includes practice activities (especially on your Launchkey), and ends with a **checkpoint** that tests the same objectives.

Within each module, work in this order:

1. **Activate** — review what you already know (even if it is only “I can find middle C”).
2. **Study** — read the concept section carefully.
3. **Demonstrate** — play or clap the examples yourself.
4. **Practice** — complete the Launchkey drills.
5. **Check** — pass the checkpoint before moving on.
6. **Transfer** — try the same skill in a second key or with a simple song idea.

Early modules emphasize **recall and comprehension** (names, patterns, locations). Later modules emphasize **application** (build chords, play progressions in time).

### What you need

- Novation **Launchkey 61 MK4** (USB-powered MIDI controller)
- A DAW or sound source (Ableton Live Lite, Cubase LE, Novation Play, or any synth that receives MIDI)
- Optional but helpful: a free online piano keyboard diagram, *Open Music Theory* chapters linked in Sources

### Practice hygiene (every session)

- Sit tall; keep wrists flexible, not locked.
- Start slow with a metronome (use Launchkey **Metronome** when integrated with a DAW, or your DAW’s click).
- Prefer accuracy over speed.
- Turn **Scale Mode** or chord assists **off** for part of each practice so you still learn the real key layout—then turn them back on as training wheels.

### Important: middle C labeling (C3 vs C4)

- On the **Launchkey 61**, power-on default range is **C1–C6**, and Novation treats **C3 as middle C**.
- Many theory textbooks and some DAWs label middle C as **C4**.
- Both refer to the same pitch (MIDI note 60, roughly 261.6 Hz). Only the **octave number** differs by convention.  
  **In this course, “middle C” means the pitch; on your Launchkey, that pitch is labeled C3.**

# Module 1 — Pitch Names and Keyboard Geography

### Learning objectives

1. Name the seven letter names of Western pitch notation.
2. Explain octave equivalence.
3. Locate C and F from black-key groups; locate middle C on a 61-key board.
4. Name any white key by sight on the Launchkey.

### Core theory

#### Letter names

In Western notation, pitches are named with the letters **A, B, C, D, E, F, G**, which then **repeat** each octave. Because of **octave equivalence**, pitches an octave apart share the same letter name (all “C”s sound like versions of the same pitch class at different heights).

#### Black-key landmarks

The black keys form alternating groups of **two** and **three**:

- The white key immediately **left of a group of two black keys** is **C**.
- The white key immediately **left of a group of three black keys** is **F**.

Memorize those two anchors; every other white key is counted from them.

#### Middle C on a standard 61-key layout

On a typical 61-key keyboard, **middle C** is the **third C from the left** (the **15th white key**), immediately left of a group of two black keys. On your Launchkey at default octave, that pitch is labeled **C3**.

#### Generic intervals (preview)

When counting distance on the keyboard or staff, **always count the first note as “one,” not zero**. Example: F up to C is a **generic fifth** (F=1, G=2, A=3, B=4, C=5).

### Launchkey practice

**Drill 1.1 — Find all Cs**  
With octave reset to default, find every C on the board. Say “C” out loud. Repeat for F.

**Drill 1.2 — White-key alphabet**  
Starting on middle C (C3), play ascending white keys only: C D E F G A B C. Descend. Then start on A (natural minor “home” for later modules) and name each key.

**Drill 1.3 — Random white-key quiz**  
Close eyes, land a finger on any white key, open eyes, name it within 2 seconds. 20 reps.

**Drill 1.4 — Octave awareness**  
Play middle C, then the C one octave higher, then one octave lower. Feel that they share a letter name but not the same pitch height.

### Checkpoint 1

- [ ] Name any white key pointed to by a friend (or random finger) within 2 seconds.
- [ ] Point to middle C and state its Launchkey label (C3 at default).
- [ ] Explain octave equivalence in one sentence.

# Module 2 — Half Steps, Whole Steps, Accidentals

### Learning objectives

1. Define half step and whole step on the keyboard.
2. Identify the white-key half steps (E–F and B–C).
3. Name black keys with sharps and flats; explain enharmonic equivalence.
4. Apply accidentals: sharp, flat, natural, double sharp, double flat.

### Core theory

#### Half steps and whole steps

- A **half step** (semitone) is the smallest interval in standard Western keyboard pitch systems: the key immediately to the **right** (up) or **left** (down) of a given key.
- A **whole step** is **two** half steps.

Most adjacent white keys have a black key between them (whole step). Two pairs of white keys are already half steps with **no** black key between them: **E–F** and **B–C**.

#### Accidentals

An **accidental** changes pitch:

| Symbol | Name | Effect |
|--------|------|--------|
| ♯ | Sharp | Raise by one half step |
| ♭ | Flat | Lower by one half step |
| ♮ | Natural | Cancel a previous accidental |
| 𝄪 | Double sharp | Raise by a whole step (two half steps) |
| 𝄫 | Double flat | Lower by a whole step |

Accidentals are written **to the left** of a notehead, on the same line or space.

#### Black-key names

- Black key a half step **above** a white key: white key name + **sharp** (C♯ is right of C).
- Black key a half step **below** a white key: white key name + **flat** (D♭ is left of D).

The same black key is often both: **C♯ = D♭**.

#### Enharmonic equivalence

Notes that are **spelled differently but sound the same** are **enharmonically equivalent** (e.g., C♯ and D♭; also E♯ = F, C♭ = B, etc.).

### Launchkey practice

**Drill 2.1 — Chromatic walk**  
From middle C, play every consecutive key upward for one octave (all twelve pitch classes). Count “half step” between each. Descend.

**Drill 2.2 — E–F and B–C**  
Play only those half-step pairs in several octaves. Say “half step” each time.

**Drill 2.3 — Name both spellings**  
For each black key, say both common names (e.g., “F sharp / G flat”).

**Drill 2.4 — Whole-step ladder**  
From C, move by whole steps only: C–D–E–F♯–G♯–A♯–C (whole-tone sound). Do not yet worry about “theory of modes”; just feel whole steps.

### Checkpoint 2

- [ ] Define half step and whole step on the keyboard.
- [ ] Point out E–F and B–C and explain why they matter for scales.
- [ ] Given any black key, give at least one sharp and one flat name when both apply.
- [ ] State what enharmonic equivalence means.

# Module 3 — Beat, Meter, and Time Signatures

### Learning objectives

1. Distinguish beat from meter.
2. Identify simple duple, triple, and quadruple meter by ear and by counting.
3. Read simple time signatures (top = beats per measure; bottom = beat unit).
4. Count basic subdivisions (“1 and 2 and…”).

### Core theory

#### Beat and meter

- A **beat** is a regularly recurring pulse (what you tap your foot to).
- **Meter** is the recurring pattern of accents that **groups** those beats (duple, triple, quadruple).

#### Simple meter

In **simple meters**, each beat divides into **two** (and further into four).

| Type | Beats per group | Feel |
|------|-----------------|------|
| Simple duple | 2 | STRONG–weak |
| Simple triple | 3 | STRONG–weak–weak |
| Simple quadruple | 4 | STRONG–weak–medium–weak (often similar to two groups of two) |

#### Measures and bar lines

A **measure** (bar) equals one full grouping of beats. **Bar lines** separate measures.

#### Time signatures (simple meters)

In simple meters, a time signature has two numbers (not a fraction—no line between them):

- **Top number:** how many beats per measure (2, 3, or 4 for simple duple/triple/quadruple).
- **Bottom number:** which note value gets the beat (2 = half note, 4 = quarter note, 8 = eighth note, etc.).

Examples:

- **4/4** — four quarter-note beats per measure (also called **common time**, //).
- **3/4** — three quarter-note beats per measure (waltz feel).
- **2/4** — two quarter-note beats per measure.
- **2/2** — two half-note beats (**cut time**, ₵).

#### Counting (American traditional style)

In 4/4 with quarter = beat:

- Beats: **1, 2, 3, 4**
- Eighths: **1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and**
- Sixteenths: **1 e and a 2 e and a…**

Silent beats (rests, held notes) are often written in parentheses when counting.

#### Downbeat and upbeat

- **Downbeat:** beat 1 of the measure (most weight).
- **Upbeat:** last beat of a measure (often anticipatory).

An **anacrusis** (pickup) starts before beat 1; counting may not begin on “1.”

### Launchkey practice

**Drill 3.1 — Pulse alone**  
Play one note on every beat with a metronome at 60–80 BPM. Steady velocity.

**Drill 3.2 — Meter clap/play**  
- Duple: accent 1 of every 2 beats.  
- Triple: accent 1 of every 3.  
- Quadruple: accent 1 of every 4 (lighter accent on 3 optional).

**Drill 3.3 — Subdivision**  
In 4/4, play eighth notes (“1 and 2 and…”) while the metronome clicks quarters.

**Drill 3.4 — DAW transport**  
Use Launchkey Play / Stop / Loop / Record and Metronome (with DAW integration) to record a 4-bar steady pulse, then review.

### Checkpoint 3

- [ ] Define beat vs meter in one sentence each.
- [ ] Clap/play duple vs triple so a listener can tell them apart.
- [ ] Explain what 4/4 and 3/4 mean.
- [ ] Count a bar of eighth notes aloud while playing.

# Module 4 — Intervals

### Learning objectives

1. Define interval; distinguish melodic vs harmonic intervals.
2. State size (generic) and quality (specific).
3. Identify perfect vs major/minor interval families.
4. Build intervals from a lower note using the major-scale method.
5. Invert simple intervals (sizes add to 9; quality flips as specified).

### Core theory

#### What is an interval?

An **interval** is the distance between two pitches.  
- **Melodic interval:** notes one after another.  
- **Harmonic interval:** notes sounding together.

Every interval has:

1. **Size** (generic): unison, 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 5th, 6th, 7th, octave… — count lines/spaces or letter steps, starting the first note as 1.  
   Accidentals do **not** change generic size (C–E, C♯–E♭ are both generic thirds).

2. **Quality** (specific): perfect (P), major (M/ma), minor (m/mi), augmented (A/+), diminished (d/°).

#### Which qualities go with which sizes?

| Perfect family | Major/minor family |
|----------------|--------------------|
| Unison, 4th, 5th, octave | 2nd, 3rd, 6th, 7th |

#### Major-scale method (recommended)

To identify an interval:

1. Find the **generic size**.
2. Imagine the **bottom note** as tonic of a major scale.
3. If the top note is **in** that major scale:
   - Unison/4th/5th/8ve → **perfect**
   - 2nd/3rd/6th/7th → **major**
4. If not, compare by half steps:  
   - Major → one half step smaller → **minor**  
   - Perfect or major → one half step larger → **augmented**  
   - Perfect or minor → one half step smaller → **diminished**

#### Relative size ladder (concept)

- Augmented is one half step larger than perfect or major.  
- Minor is one half step smaller than major.  
- Diminished is one half step smaller than perfect or minor.

#### Compound intervals

Larger than an octave: add **7** to the simple size (3rd → 10th, 5th → 12th). Quality stays the same family (major 3rd → major 10th).

#### Intervallic inversion

Flip the notes (raise the lower note an octave, or lower the upper note):

- Sizes add to **9** (3rd ↔ 6th, 4th ↔ 5th, 2nd ↔ 7th, unison ↔ octave).
- Perfect ↔ perfect  
- Major ↔ minor  
- Augmented ↔ diminished  

Example: major 3rd inverts to minor 6th.

#### Consonance and dissonance (beginner awareness)

Categories vary by historical style, but a common modern teaching table includes:

- Often treated as **harmonically consonant:** major/minor 3rds and 6ths; P1, P8, P5.  
- Often treated as **harmonically dissonant:** major/minor 2nds and 7ths; augmented/diminished intervals; and frequently the **perfect 4th** in two-voice contexts.

Use this as orientation, not as an absolute law for all music.

### Launchkey practice

**Drill 4.1 — Generic sizes from C**  
Play C with right thumb; with another finger play D, E, F, G, A, B, C (2nd through octave). Name each size.

**Drill 4.2 — Major 3rd vs minor 3rd**  
C–E (M3), C–E♭ (m3). Then G–B vs G–B♭. Sing or hum after playing.

**Drill 4.3 — Perfect 5th**  
C–G, D–A, E–B, F–C. Feel the “open” quality.

**Drill 4.4 — Invert**  
Play C–E (M3), then E–C (m6). Say the inversion relationship.

**Drill 4.5 — Scale Mode assist (optional)**  
Turn Scale Mode on (Major, root C), Easy Scale, and explore only in-scale intervals between white keys.

### Checkpoint 4

- [ ] Define size vs quality.
- [ ] Identify M3, m3, P5, P4 from C by ear and on the keys.
- [ ] Invert a major 3rd and state the result.
- [ ] Explain the major-scale method in three steps.

# Module 5 — The Major Scale

### Learning objectives

1. State the major-scale step pattern: **W–W–H–W–W–W–H**.
2. Build a major scale from any tonic.
3. Name scale degrees (numbers, movable solfège, degree names).
4. Play C major and at least two other major scales ascending and descending on the Launchkey.

### Core theory

#### Definition

A **major scale** is an ordered collection of whole and half steps with the ascending pattern:

**W – W – H – W – W – W – H**

It is named for its first (and last) note, including any accidental (e.g., B♭ major, not “B major with flats” as a name).

#### Scale degrees, solfège, and degree names

| Degree | Solfège (movable *do*) | Degree name |
|--------|------------------------|-------------|
| 1̂ | *do* | Tonic |
| 2̂ | *re* | Supertonic |
| 3̂ | *mi* | Mediant |
| 4̂ | *fa* | Subdominant |
| 5̂ | *sol* | Dominant |
| 6̂ | *la* | Submediant |
| 7̂ | *ti* | Leading tone |
| 1̂/8̂ | *do* | Tonic |

**Movable *do*:** *do* is the tonic of the current major scale (not always C).  
**Fixed *do*:** *do* is always C — a different system; this course uses movable *do* for thinking in keys.

#### Why E–F and B–C matter

In C major (all white keys), the half steps fall exactly on **E–F** (3–4) and **B–C** (7–1), matching W–W–H–W–W–W–H.

### Launchkey practice

**Drill 5.1 — C major hands separately**  
RH ascending/descending one octave; then LH. Name degrees out loud (*do re mi…* or 1 2 3…).

**Drill 5.2 — Build from pattern**  
Pick G: G–A–B–C–D–E–F♯–G. Verify each step is W or H as required. Repeat from F: F–G–A–B♭–C–D–E–F.

**Drill 5.3 — Scale Mode (training wheels)**  
1. Press **Scale**.  
2. Encoder 1: root.  
3. Encoder 2: **Major**.  
4. Encoder 3: try modes:  
   - **Snap to Scale** — out-of-scale notes snap to nearest in-scale note.  
   - **Filter out of Scale** — out-of-scale keys silent.  
   - **Easy Scale** — scale notes mapped to white keys.  

Practice: play freely while locked to Major; then turn Scale Mode **off** and play the same scale “for real.”

**Drill 5.4 — Degrees as targets**  
Play only 1̂, 3̂, 5̂ (tonic triad preview) in C major, then the full scale.

### Checkpoint 5

- [ ] Recite W–W–H–W–W–W–H from memory.
- [ ] Play C major up and down cleanly at a slow tempo.
- [ ] Build and play G major and F major from the pattern (with correct F♯ / B♭).
- [ ] Name tonic, dominant, and leading tone in any major scale you play.

# Module 6 — Key Signatures and the Circle of Fifths

### Learning objectives

1. Explain what a key signature does and where it appears (after clef, before time signature).
2. State the order of sharps and flats.
3. Identify major keys from sharp and flat signatures using standard tricks.
4. Navigate related keys on the circle of fifths.

### Core theory

#### What a key signature is

A **key signature** is a fixed set of sharps or flats placed after the clef and before the time signature. It marks which pitch classes are altered throughout the piece (in every octave), encoding the accidentals of a major scale (or its relative minor).

Alphabetical memory aid for order on the page: **clef → key → time**.

#### Order of accidentals

- **Sharps:** F C G D A E B  
  Mnemonic: *Fat Cats Go Down Alleys (to) Eat Birds*
- **Flats:** B E A D G C F (reverse of sharps)  
  Mnemonic: *Birds Eat And Dive Going Copiously Far*

#### Identifying major keys

**Sharp keys:** the **last sharp** is a half step below the tonic.  
Example: last sharp F♯ → tonic G → G major.

**Flat keys:** the **second-to-last flat** is the tonic.  
Example: flats B♭, E♭ → second-to-last is B♭ → B♭ major.

**Memorize specially:**

- **C major:** no sharps or flats  
- **F major:** one flat (B♭)

#### Major key signatures in order

**Sharps (add one each time):**  
C (0) → G (1) → D (2) → A (3) → E (4) → B (5) → F♯ (6) → C♯ (7)

**Flats:**  
C (0) → F (1) → B♭ (2) → E♭ (3) → A♭ (4) → D♭ (5) → G♭ (6) → C♭ (7)

#### Circle of fifths

Major keys arranged so each step clockwise is a fifth up (one more sharp), counterclockwise a fifth down (one more flat). Bottom keys may be **enharmonically equivalent** pairs (e.g., B major with 5 sharps vs C♭ major with 7 flats sound the same collection of pitch classes when spelled enharmonically).

### Launchkey practice

**Drill 6.1 — One accidental at a time**  
Play G major (F♯). Then D major (F♯, C♯). Say the key name and list the sharps.

**Drill 6.2 — Scale Mode + Filter**  
Set root and Major scale for G, D, F, B♭. Use **Filter out of Scale** and try to play wrong notes on purpose—they should not sound.

**Drill 6.3 — Signature → scale**  
Without looking: “two sharps” → play D major. “three flats” → play E♭ major.

**Drill 6.4 — Circle walk**  
Play tonics only around the circle of fifths upward: C–G–D–A–E–B–F♯… then reverse with flats.

### Checkpoint 6

- [ ] State order of sharps and flats.
- [ ] Given “last sharp is D♯,” name the major key (E major).
- [ ] Given flats B♭ E♭ A♭, name the major key (E♭ major).
- [ ] Play scales for C, G, D, F, and B♭ major.

# Module 7 — Minor Scales and Relative Keys

### Learning objectives

1. State that a minor scale’s third is a half step lower than the parallel major’s third.
2. Build natural, harmonic, and melodic minor patterns.
3. Explain relative vs parallel major/minor relationships.
4. Know that minor **key signatures** follow natural minor / relative major.

### Core theory

#### Three forms of minor (one “key,” three common scale flavors)

Think of them as related patterns used in practice and writing—not as three separate “key types” on sheet music. Pieces are simply “in minor”; key signatures are based on **natural minor**.

**Natural minor (ascending):**  
**W – H – W – W – H – W – W**

**Harmonic minor (ascending):**  
**W – H – W – W – H – 3 half steps – H**  
(raised 7̂ creates a leading tone; the step from 6̂ to 7̂ is an augmented second / three half steps)

**Melodic minor:**  
- **Ascending:** W – H – W – W – W – W – H (raised 6̂ and 7̂)  
- **Descending:** same as natural minor (W – W – H – W – W – H – W)

#### Compared with parallel major

| Form | Lowered degrees vs parallel major |
|------|-----------------------------------|
| Natural | 3̂, 6̂, 7̂ |
| Harmonic | 3̂, 6̂ (7̂ raised) |
| Melodic ascending | 3̂ only (6̂ and 7̂ raised) |

#### Solfège changes in minor (movable *do*)

Natural minor uses *me*, *le*, *te* (lowered 3, 6, 7). Harmonic minor restores *ti*. Melodic ascending uses *la–ti*; descending returns to *te–le*.

#### Scale-degree names in minor

Mostly same as major; lowered 7̂ is the **subtonic** (*te*), while raised 7̂ is the **leading tone** (*ti*).

#### Parallel vs relative

- **Parallel:** same tonic (C major / C minor).  
- **Relative:** same key signature; minor tonic is **three half steps below** major tonic (C major ↔ A minor; F major ↔ D minor).

### Launchkey practice

**Drill 7.1 — A natural minor**  
All white keys A–B–C–D–E–F–G–A. Compare to C major (same white keys, different tonic).

**Drill 7.2 — A harmonic minor**  
Raise G to G♯: A B C D E F G♯ A. Hear the “Middle Eastern / classical minor” pull of the augmented second F–G♯.

**Drill 7.3 — A melodic minor**  
Up: A B C D E F♯ G♯ A. Down: A G F E D C B A.

**Drill 7.4 — Scale Mode**  
Encoder 2: try **Minor**, **Harmonic Minor**. (Melodic minor appears among scale families in Chord Map documentation as a related scale set; use the scale list on Encoder 2 for what is available on your unit.)

**Drill 7.5 — Relative pairs**  
Play C major then A minor; G major then E minor; F major then D minor.

### Checkpoint 7

- [ ] Recite natural minor pattern W–H–W–W–H–W–W.
- [ ] Play A natural, harmonic, and melodic minor.
- [ ] State relative minor of C, G, and F major.
- [ ] Explain parallel vs relative in one sentence each.

# Module 8 — Triads

### Learning objectives

1. Define a triad as three pitch classes stackable as thirds.
2. Build major, minor, diminished, and augmented triads from interval recipes.
3. Play root-position triads with RH fingers 1–3–5.
4. Label triads with lead-sheet symbols and Roman numerals in major.

### Core theory

#### Chord and triad

A **chord** is any combination of three or more pitch classes sounding together.  
A **triad** is a three-note chord whose pitch classes can be arranged as stacked thirds: **root, third, fifth**.

#### Four triad qualities

| Quality | Intervals above root | Sound shorthand |
|---------|----------------------|-----------------|
| Major | M3 + P5 | *do–mi–sol* |
| Minor | m3 + P5 | *do–me–sol* |
| Diminished | m3 + d5 | *ti–re–fa* type |
| Augmented | M3 + A5 | *me–sol–ti* type |

#### Lead-sheet symbols (root + quality)

- Major: **C** (no extra quality mark)  
- Minor: **Cm**  
- Diminished: **Cdim** or **C°**  
- Augmented: **Caug** or **C+**  
- Slash: **Cm/E♭** means C minor triad with E♭ in the bass (inversion)

#### Inversion (preview)

When a note other than the root is lowest, the triad is **inverted**. Chord Map’s inversion performance pads cycle inversions—use that after you understand root position.

#### Close vs open position

- **Close:** chord tones as close as possible above the bass.  
- **Open:** spaces between chord tones.

#### Roman numerals in major keys (quality-aware)

In any major key, diatonic triads:

| RN | Quality | Scale degrees |
|----|---------|---------------|
| I | Major | 1–3–5 |
| ii | Minor | 2–4–6 |
| iii | Minor | 3–5–7 |
| IV | Major | 4–6–1 |
| V | Major | 5–7–2 |
| vi | Minor | 6–1–3 |
| vii° | Diminished | 7–2–4 |

(Capital = major; lowercase = minor; ° = diminished.)

#### Hand shape for beginners

Root-position triads are commonly played RH **1–3–5** (thumb–middle–pinky), a transferable shape for skipping every other scale tone.

### Launchkey practice

**Drill 8.1 — Four qualities from C**  
- C: C–E–G  
- Cm: C–E♭–G  
- Cdim: C–E♭–G♭  
- Caug: C–E–G♯  

Use RH 1–3–5. Name each quality.

**Drill 8.2 — Diatonic triads in C major**  
Play I–ii–iii–IV–V–vi–vii°–I as root-position triads (white keys only for C major).

**Drill 8.3 — Chord Detector**  
Play triads and read the chord name on the OLED (Chord Detector).

**Drill 8.4 — Fixed Chord**  
1. Hold **Fixed Chord**.  
2. Play C–E–G (or enter notes one by one, up to six notes).  
3. Release Fixed Chord.  
4. Press single keys up the keyboard: the same **interval shape** transposes.  
Great for internalizing a hand shape across keys.

**Drill 8.5 — Chord Map**  
1. Set Scale Mode root/scale (e.g., C Major).  
2. Press **Chord Map**.  
3. Left **eight blue pads** = scale-related chords.  
4. Hold right **performance pads** for Manual Arp, Inversions, Split modes.  
5. Encoders: Adventure / Explore (banks), Spread / Roll (voicing feel).

**Default note:** Chord modes may send on MIDI channel 3 by default (Settings → Chords Channel).

**Drill 8.6 — User Chord**  
Hold a blank pad, play a triad (up to six notes), release. Build a pad bank of I–IV–V–vi for a song key. Transpose the bank with pad up/down (semitone; Shift+ for octave).

### Checkpoint 8

- [ ] Build all four triad qualities from C and from G.
- [ ] Play all diatonic triads in C major, naming Roman numerals.
- [ ] Play a root-position major triad RH 1–3–5 in three keys.
- [ ] Demonstrate Fixed Chord or Chord Map producing a named triad.

# Module 9 — Seventh Chords

### Learning objectives

1. Define a seventh chord as four notes stackable as thirds (root–3rd–5th–7th).
2. Identify the five common seventh qualities by interval recipe.
3. Play major 7, dominant 7, and minor 7 from common roots.
4. Locate V7 in a major key.

### Core theory

#### Seventh chord definition

A **seventh chord** adds a fourth pitch class a third above the fifth: **root, third, fifth, seventh**.

#### Five common qualities

| Quality | Intervals above root | Recipe |
|---------|----------------------|--------|
| Major seventh | M3, P5, M7 | Major triad + major 7th |
| Dominant seventh | M3, P5, m7 | Major triad + minor 7th |
| Minor seventh | m3, P5, m7 | Minor triad + minor 7th |
| Half-diminished seventh | m3, d5, m7 | Diminished triad + minor 7th |
| Fully diminished seventh | m3, d5, d7 | Diminished triad + diminished 7th |

#### Lead-sheet abbreviations

- Major 7: **Cmaj7** or **C△7**  
- Dominant 7: **C7**  
- Minor 7: **Cm7**  
- Diminished 7: **Cdim7** / **C°7**  
- Half-diminished: **Cø7**

#### Diatonic sevenths in major (Roman numerals)

| RN | Quality |
|----|---------|
| I7 | Major seventh |
| ii7 | Minor seventh |
| iii7 | Minor seventh |
| IV7 | Major seventh |
| V7 | Dominant seventh |
| vi7 | Minor seventh |
| viiø7 | Half-diminished seventh |

**V7** (dominant seventh) is especially important: in C major, **G–B–D–F**.

### Launchkey practice

**Drill 9.1 — C family**  
- Cmaj7: C E G B  
- C7: C E G B♭  
- Cm7: C E♭ G B♭  

Compare the seventh of maj7 vs dominant 7.

**Drill 9.2 — V7 in C, G, F**  
- C: G7  
- G: D7  
- F: C7  

**Drill 9.3 — Arpeggiate sevenths**  
Hold a seventh chord with Arp on (Type: Up; Rate: 1/8 or 1/16). Hear each chord tone in sequence.

**Drill 9.4 — User Chord bank**  
Store Imaj7–IV–V7–vi for C major on four pads; play a simple loop with the metronome.

### Checkpoint 9

- [ ] Build Cmaj7, C7, and Cm7 and name the difference.
- [ ] Play V7 in C major (G7) and resolve to I (C).
- [ ] Identify which Roman numeral is the dominant seventh in major (V7).

# Module 10 — Rhythm in Practice: Arpeggiator and Patterns

### Learning objectives

1. Explain what an arpeggiator does.
2. Control tempo, rate, gate, type, octaves, swing.
3. Use Arp Pattern mode basics (step on/off, accent, ratchet, tie).
4. Apply arpeggios to scales and chords you already know.

### Core theory + hardware

An **arpeggiator** holds your notes and plays them as a timed sequence.

#### Enable and controls (encoders when Arp is on)

| Encoder | Parameter | Default (per manual) |
|---------|-----------|----------------------|
| 1 | Tempo (BPM) | 120 (when not external MIDI clock) |
| 2 | Swing | 0 (−80% to +80%) |
| 3 | Rate | 1/16 |
| 4 | Gate | 50% (0–95%) |
| 5 | Type | Up |
| 6 | Octaves | 1 (up to 4) |
| 7 | Mutate | 0% |
| 8 | Rhythm | 0 |

**Arp types:** Up, Down, Up/Down, Up/Down 2, As Played, Random, Chord, Strum (modulation wheel “strums” held notes).

**Shift + Arp:** latch (hands-free experimentation).

#### Rates available

1/4, 1/4 triplet, 1/8, 1/8 triplet, 1/16, 1/16 triplet, 1/32, 1/32 triplet.

#### Arp Pattern mode

Hold **Shift** and press **Drum Pad 12**.  
- Top row: enable/disable steps.  
- Bottom row + **Function** cycles: **Accent** (+30 velocity, max 127), **Ratchet** (double triggers), **Tie** (connects steps; gate to 110%).

### Launchkey practice

**Drill 10.1 — Triad arpeggio**  
Hold C–E–G, Arp Type Up, Rate 1/8, one octave. Then Type Down; then Up/Down.

**Drill 10.2 — Scale tones**  
Hold 1–3–5–7 of C major (C E G B); hear maj7 arpeggio.

**Drill 10.3 — Meter connection**  
Set Rate 1/4 and play so each step lands on the beat; then 1/8 for subdivisions.

**Drill 10.4 — Swing awareness**  
At 1/16 rate, move Swing positive and negative; feel offbeats shift.

### Checkpoint 10

- [ ] Latch an arpeggio and change Type and Rate deliberately.
- [ ] Explain gate vs rate in one sentence each.
- [ ] Create a simple 4-step on/off pattern in Arp Pattern mode.

# Module 11 — Putting It Together: Progressions and Song Skills

### Learning objectives

1. Play common major-key progressions with triads (and optional sevenths).
2. Keep steady meter while changing chords.
3. Transfer one progression to a second key.
4. Use Split mode for bass + chords (optional).

### Core theory (practical harmony layer)

Without full functional theory, beginners can still use:

- **I – IV – V – I** (classical/basic rock foundation)  
- **I – V – vi – IV** (very common pop loop)  
- **ii – V – I** (jazz/pop cadence seed; use triads or sevenths)

In **C major**:

| RN | Chord |
|----|-------|
| I | C |
| ii | Dm |
| IV | F |
| V | G |
| vi | Am |

**V → I** feels like resolution (dominant to tonic).  
**vi** feels relative minor color inside a major key.

### Launchkey practice

**Drill 11.1 — I–V–vi–IV in C**  
C – G – Am – F, one bar each, RH triads, LH roots or octaves. Metronome 70–90 BPM.

**Drill 11.2 — Same progression in G**  
G – D – Em – C.

**Drill 11.3 — Chord Map performance**  
Use Inversion Up/Down pads to vary voicings without changing the progression.

**Drill 11.4 — Split bass/chords**  
Shift + Fader 8 (Split). Set split near C3. Left hand: roots. Right hand: triads (or Chord Map on a different channel setup as needed).

**Drill 11.5 — Capture and review**  
Record 4–8 bars with Capture MIDI / Record; listen back for steady time and correct chord tones.

### Checkpoint 11 (summative)

- [ ] Play I–V–vi–IV in C major for 8 bars with steady quarter-note pulse.
- [ ] Transpose the same progression to G major.
- [ ] Name each chord with letter name **and** Roman numeral.
- [ ] Optionally: add V7 before I at a cadence (G7 → C).

# Module 12 — Scale Mode Catalog (Launchkey) for Further Exploration

### Learning objectives

1. Know that Scale Mode includes a documented list of scales on Encoder 2.
2. Use modes carefully: theory names match, but treat advanced modes as **ear exploration** until you study them formally.

### Scales listed in the official Scale Mode table

Major · Minor · Dorian · Mixolydian · Lydian · Phrygian · Locrian · Whole Tone · Half Whole Dim · Whole Half Diminished · Blues · Minor Pentatonic · Major Pentatonic · Harmonic Minor · Harmonic Major  

Product materials also state **30 scales** total; the user-guide table above is the set shown in the official “Scales” table. Chord Map documentation references additional named scale families (e.g., Melodic Minor, Phrygian Dominant, various world scales) paired with chord sets—use the OLED and Encoder 2 as the authority for what is loaded on your firmware.

### Beginner-friendly next scales

After major and natural minor:

1. **Major pentatonic** — 1 2 3 5 6 (great for solos; Easy Scale runs).  
2. **Minor pentatonic** — classic rock/blues “safe” set.  
3. **Blues** — minor pentatonic flavor with blue note color.  
4. **Dorian / Mixolydian** — modes of the major scale (study formally when ready: same step patterns as segments of major, different tonic).

### Practice

Lock Scale Mode to Major Pentatonic, root C, Easy Scale: improvise only on white keys for 2 minutes. Then turn Scale Mode off and find the same notes on the real black/white layout.

# Known limits and honesty notes

These are intentional boundaries so the document does not overclaim:

1. **No universal “only correct” beginner order** exists; the sequence here follows conventional Western fundamentals as ordered in *Open Music Theory*, not a proven single cognitive ladder.
2. **Harmony as full phrase-level function** (tonic–predominant–dominant analysis) is only introduced practically in Module 11; deeper functional harmony is a later course.
3. **Full enumeration of all 30 Launchkey scales** may exceed the partial table in the user guide; trust your unit’s Encoder 2 list and firmware.
4. **Chord Detector** naming rules for every inversion/voicing are not fully specified in the sources used; use it as feedback, not as the sole authority for analysis.
5. **Octave numbering** of middle C differs (Launchkey C3 vs many texts C4); the pitch is the same.
6. **Aftertouch:** pads yes (polyphonic); keys—not claimed as aftertouch in inspected official materials.
7. **Melodics** lessons bundled with Launchkey were not audited for this document’s content.
8. Practice times and “8-week path” are **suggested scaffolds**, not prescriptions from a controlled study.
